Velieta, Icyglee, BODEGA, Ca'Lefort: which 24-inch cabinet earns its price?

All four of these are full 24-inch cabinets sized to fill a base-cabinet opening, not the countertop units we compare elsewhere on this site. That changes the question from "will it fit" to "what does the extra depth, and the extra few hundred dollars, actually buy." We read what each listing states outright — bottle count, zones, installation type — at four prices from $579.99 to $1,399.99.

The short version

All four are genuine 24-inch cabinets — the width that fills a standard base-cabinet opening — not the 12-to-18-inch countertop units people usually mean when they ask "which brand is best." That single fact changes what matters: at this size, zones, bottle count and installation type do real work, and the four brands state very different amounts of it.

Icyglee states the most useful number outright — 54 bottles, dual zone, $579.99, the cheapest of the four. Velieta is the most expensive at $1,399.99 and the vaguest: the listing leads with "Professional Large Capacity" and never gives a bottle count. BODEGA and Ca'Lefort sit in between, each with one spec worth checking closely before you buy.

If you only read one section, make it how to read the spec sheet instead of the brand name — the discrepancy in Ca'Lefort's own listing is the clearest example on this page of why that matters.

Why this is a different comparison than the compact-unit one

We've run brand-versus-brand comparisons on this site before, and the previous one was built entirely around compact, countertop-sized units — the 12-to-15-bottle cabinets people buy for a kitchen counter or a bar cart. This one is not that. Every product below is a 24-inch-wide cabinet, the width built to slide into a standard base-cabinet cutout or stand as a full-size freestanding unit next to a refrigerator. That is a different category of purchase: more bottles, more weight, more up-front cost, and — because several of these are rated or marketed for built-in use — a decision that can be expensive to undo if you enclose the wrong one.

At countertop size, the questions that matter most are noise and whether the thing fits on a counter at all. At 24 inches, the questions shift: how many bottles does the cabinet actually state it holds, is that number consistent everywhere the brand prints it, does it run one temperature zone or two, and can it be built into a cabinet run or does it need open air on all sides. Those are exactly the four questions where Velieta, Icyglee, BODEGA and Ca'Lefort answer very differently — and, in one case, inconsistently with themselves.

Velieta: the capacity-first catalog, and where the price climbs fastest

Velieta's listing title for this cabinet reads like a checklist of selling points: "Professional Large Capacity High Wine Refrigerator with Powerful Compressor, Quiet Operation and Elegant Design." Count the adjectives and you get five — professional, large, high, powerful, elegant — and one concrete number: zero. Dual zone is stated directly, and so is compressor cooling ("Powerful Compressor"), which is a genuinely useful confirmation, since compressor units hold a setpoint regardless of what the room is doing, where a thermoelectric unit would not. What the title does not state, anywhere, is how many bottles the cabinet holds.

The one additional detail in our database for this listing — its size variant — doesn't fill that gap either. It reads "24 inch Depth," describing a physical dimension rather than a capacity figure. That's notable on its own: for the other three brands in this comparison, the size-variant field is where a bottle count or zone configuration shows up. For Velieta, it's where a depth measurement shows up instead. Between the title and the variant data, there is no bottle count anywhere in what this listing commits to in writing.

And this is the most expensive cabinet in the whole comparison by a wide margin: $1,399.99, more than double BODEGA's $659.99 and nearly a thousand dollars above Icyglee's $579.99. A compressor-cooled, dual-zone, full-size cabinet can absolutely be worth that premium — but "Professional Large Capacity" is a phrase, not a specification, and at this price the capacity number is exactly what you'd want confirmed before paying it. If a quiet mechanical room matters to you as much as capacity does, it's also worth knowing that "Quiet Operation" on a compressor unit is a comparative claim, not a silence claim — compressor units cycle on and off audibly by design, which is the whole reason some buyers choose a genuinely silent thermoelectric cabinet for a room where that matters more than cooling power in a warm space.

Icyglee: the mid-size dual-zone value play at 54 bottles

Icyglee's listing is the most straightforward of the four. The title states the width (24 inches), the capacity (54 bottles), the configuration (dual zone) and the finish (silver) in one line, with nothing left for us to infer or flag as unstated. At $579.99 it is also the cheapest cabinet in this comparison, and the only one of the four where we can do clean bottle-for-dollar math without a caveat: $579.99 across 54 bottles works out to roughly $10.74 per bottle of stated capacity, the lowest figure on this page.

Fifty-four bottles in a 24-inch dual-zone cabinet lands squarely in the range covered by our guide to wine fridges between 50 and 80 bottles — big enough to stop being a kitchen accessory and start functioning as real storage for two people who drink regularly, small enough to still plausibly fit against a wall rather than needing a dedicated room. Dual zone at this size is a genuinely useful split rather than a decorative one: enough bottles per side that neither compartment is left holding four bottles rattling around a half-empty shelf.

What the listing does not state is installation type or cooling technology — no "built-in or freestanding," no "compressor" or "thermoelectric" anywhere in the title. Given the dual-zone claim and the price point, compressor cooling is the likely bet (thermoelectric dual-zone units at full 24-inch size are uncommon, since thermoelectric's roughly 20°F-below-ambient ceiling makes a wide zone spread hard to deliver), but that's an inference from general category patterns, not a number this specific listing confirms. If built-in installation matters to you, check the listing's own installation field before you buy rather than assuming either way.

BODEGA: the wine-and-beverage hybrid, built into the same width

BODEGA's listing is the most detailed of the four, and it's selling a different kind of cabinet entirely: "Wine and Beverage Refrigerator," "Dual Zone Wine and Bar Beverage Cooler," "Commercial Black Bar Cooler With 2 Glass Doors," holding "19 Bottles and 57 Cans," rated "Built-In or Freestanding." That's five separate, checkable claims in one title — more than any other listing in this comparison states outright — and it describes a cabinet built for a different job than Icyglee's all-wine 54-bottle unit at the same 24-inch width.

The trade is the one we've pointed out elsewhere on this site whenever a wine-and-beverage hybrid comes up: the can side of the cabinet is real, usable space that an all-wine cabinet would have racked for bottles instead. Nineteen bottles is a little more than a third of what Icyglee's 54-bottle unit holds in the identical 24-inch footprint — the rest of BODEGA's interior went to 57 cans of beer or soda at drinking temperature. That's an excellent trade if your actual problem is bottles and cans competing for the same fridge space; it's the wrong cabinet if what you want is maximum wine storage and you'd open the holiday guide for occasions like entertaining a crowd that drinks both.

Two glass doors, stated directly, is a meaningful design detail: it means the wine side and the beverage side each get their own door and their own seal, rather than one door spanning the full 24-inch width. More doors generally means more gasket to maintain over the cabinet's life — not a flaw, just a detail worth knowing if you ever run into the kind of leveling or seal issue covered in our guide to a wine fridge door that won't stay closed. "Commercial" in the title is a brand descriptor, not a certification — if you specifically need a unit certified for restaurant or bar service, confirm that directly with BODEGA or the listing's documentation rather than inferring it from the word alone.

Ca'Lefort: built-in, and where its 51-bottle unit actually sits on price

Ca'Lefort's title is comparatively plain: "24 Inch Wine Fridge, 51-Bottle Built-In Wine Cooler." Two numbers worth noting immediately — the stated capacity of 51 bottles, and "Built-In," the only brand in this comparison besides BODEGA to state an installation type directly in the title. Unlike BODEGA's "or Freestanding," Ca'Lefort's title doesn't offer a freestanding option; treat this as a built-in-rated cabinet unless the listing's own installation notes say otherwise.

Here's the detail worth slowing down for. Our product database's size-variant field for this exact listing reads "24 Inch - Dual Zone 52 Bottle(5.65 cu.ft)" — one bottle more than the title, and a zone configuration (dual zone) the title never mentions at all. Both figures come from Ca'Lefort's own listing; they simply disagree with each other, in the way that a product's main title and its specific size/variant selector sometimes do when a brand lists closely related SKUs under one listing. At $719.88, that's roughly $14.11 per bottle using the title's 51, or $13.84 using the variant's 52 — not a meaningful difference in practice, especially once you factor in that advertised capacities everywhere in this category run optimistic against real-world bottle shapes. But it is the clearest reminder on this page that the number in a product title and the number in that same product's own spec fields aren't guaranteed to match, and the gap costs nothing to check before you buy.

What we can't confirm from the data we have is finish. Icyglee states "Silver" and BODEGA states "Commercial Black" directly in their titles; Ca'Lefort's title and variant data give us bottle count, zone configuration and cubic footage, but no stated color or finish. If a specific look matters — brushed steel, black, stainless — verify it against the listing's own photos rather than assuming, the same caution that applies to every spec this comparison flags as unstated. At 5.65 cubic feet for roughly 51–52 bottles, this is also a reasonably dense use of interior space for a built-in dual-zone cabinet, a number worth keeping in mind if you're weighing it against a cabinet destined for a basement or cellar room where interior efficiency matters more than it does in a showcase kitchen.

How to read the spec sheet instead of the brand name

Lay the four listings side by side and a pattern shows up that has nothing to do with which brand is "better" and everything to do with which brand states more in writing. Velieta leads with adjectives and gives you compressor cooling and dual zone, but no bottle count anywhere — at the highest price on this page. Icyglee states capacity, zones and finish in one line and nothing more. BODEGA states five separate specs, more than any brand here, and is the only one to confirm installation flexibility outright. Ca'Lefort states a bottle count in its title and a different bottle count — plus a zone configuration the title omits entirely — in its own size-variant field.

None of that makes one brand dishonest and another honest. It makes the brand name on its own a bad way to shop. The practical version of this lesson, for these four or any other 24-inch wine fridge: check the stated bottle count against every place the brand prints it, not just the title; check whether a zone configuration is stated at all, and where; check installation type explicitly before you assume a cabinet can be enclosed — building in a freestanding-only unit is one of the more expensive mistakes in this category; and check the listing's own photos for finish and color if that detail isn't in the text. The title is marketing copy written to be skimmed. The size-variant fields, specification tables and photos are where a brand actually commits to something you can hold it to.

Frequently asked questions

Which of these four 24-inch wine fridges holds the most bottles?

Icyglee states 54 bottles plainly and is the only one of the four with a single, unambiguous number. Ca'Lefort states 51 bottles in its title and 52 in its own size-variant data, so treat that as "roughly 51–52." BODEGA holds 19 bottles plus 57 cans — fewer bottles than either all-wine cabinet, because part of its interior is built for cans instead. Velieta's listing does not state a bottle count anywhere we could find.

Why is Velieta so much more expensive than the other three?

We can't fully answer that from the listing, and that's part of the point. The title confirms dual-zone configuration and compressor cooling, both genuine value drivers, but gives no bottle count to weigh against the $1,399.99 price — more than double BODEGA's $659.99 and nearly a thousand dollars above Icyglee's $579.99. Check the listing's specification tab for the capacity figure before deciding whether that premium is justified for your use.

Is a wine-and-beverage hybrid like BODEGA a good substitute for an all-wine cabinet like Icyglee?

Only if you actually want can storage. In the same 24-inch width, BODEGA's 19 bottles is roughly a third of Icyglee's 54-bottle capacity — the rest of the interior went to 57 cans at drinking temperature. It's an excellent choice if bottles and cans are genuinely competing for space in your kitchen; it's the wrong cabinet if the goal is maximum wine storage.

Does it matter that Ca'Lefort's title and listing disagree on bottle count — 51 versus 52?

Not by much on its own: a one-bottle gap is smaller than the real-world slack most buyers should plan for anyway, since advertised capacities across this entire category run optimistic against real bottle shapes. What it does matter for is the habit it should put you in — check a brand's full listing, including its size-variant and specification fields, rather than taking the headline number as the whole story.
